Mental Training in Chinese Sports: What Western Athletes Can Learn About Qi & Focus

By Lin Hai · 22-year Chinese sports journalist · Updated October 7, 2026 · 5 min read

For decades, Western coverage of Chinese sport zoomed in on one thing: training volume. Early mornings, long hours, huge talent pools. All true. But the quieter advantage is mental — a framework that Chinese coaches and athletes have used for generations, built on qi, jing-shen, and breath. As someone who spent 22 years reporting on Chinese sport and later trained in mindfulness, I want to translate it plainly.

What "qi" means in sport

Strip away the mysticism and qi in sport is the trained coordination of breath, attention, and movement — the feeling of being physically and mentally "switched on" and flowing. A table-tennis player who looks unhurried under pressure isn't relaxed because the moment is easy; he has rehearsed staying coordinated when it isn't.

Jing-shen (精气神): the three treasures

Chinese coaches talk constantly about jing-shen — essence, energy, and spirit. It describes an athlete's overall presence: Is he composed? Does he carry drive? Is his attention sharp? You'll hear "精气神起来了" (his spirit is up) after a good session. It's a coaching language for the whole-person state that Western sport psychology reaches by other routes.

Breath and visualization

Watch a Chinese diver on the platform, or a free-throw shooter, and you'll see a small, private routine: a slow exhale, a fixed gaze, a rehearsed motion done in the mind. That is breath plus visualization doing exactly what sports science recommends — down-regulating the nervous system and pre-loading the movement. It is the same mechanism as qigong, applied under a stopwatch.

The "stillness in movement" idea

A recurring phrase is "动中有静" — stillness inside movement. The body works hard; the attention stays quiet. Western athletes know this intuitively as "being in the zone." The Chinese framework just names it, trains it deliberately, and starts from breath rather than from hype.

What Western athletes can borrow

  • The 3-minute breath reset: before competition, slow exhales into the lower belly to steady nerves.
  • Attention anchoring: pick one physical cue (a grip, a spot on the floor) to return to when the mind wanders.
  • The "qi-ball" focus drill: imagine a soft ball of energy between the hands; when it feels solid, the attention is where you want it.

None of this needs special equipment or belief. It needs practice — which is the whole point of gong (skill built through practice).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is qi in sports?

In sport, qi is not mystical. It is the trained coordination of breath, attention, and movement — the feeling of being physically and mentally "switched on" and flowing.

Do Chinese athletes really use qigong?

Many use qigong-style breath and stillness drills, especially in precision sports like diving, archery, and table tennis, to steady nerves before competition.

What does jing-shen mean?

Jing-shen (精气神) means essence, energy, and spirit — the three treasures. Coaches use it to describe an athlete's overall presence, drive, and composure.

Can Western athletes benefit?

Yes. A 3-minute breath reset, attention anchoring, and a simple focus drill before performance are easy to adopt and need no special equipment.
